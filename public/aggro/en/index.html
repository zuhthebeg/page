01Why this tool exists

KAIST × Max Planck Institute · Aug 2026
"The goal wasn't to help either side win — it was permanent internal division."

Researchers at KAIST and the Max Planck Institute analyzed 20 years (2006–2025) of comments on Korea's largest news portal with AI and identified ~24,000 suspected foreign-linked manipulation accounts. The striking finding: they didn't back one political side. They attacked both sides at once to amplify polarization — the textbook pattern of cognitive warfare seen in the US, Taiwan, and Europe.

02What this tool does (and doesn't)

The researchers caught those accounts through network behavior — election-time activity spikes, coordinated timing, graph connections. No tool can look at a single post and tell you whether its author is a paid operative. So this one doesn't pretend to.

Instead it detects the linguistic fingerprint that study documented — extreme moral condemnation, blind glorification, us-vs-them splitting, engineered outrage — in the text itself. Not "who wrote this," but "how is this trying to steer me."

03Four layers of digital immunity

👁️ Perspective checkThe loudest extremes online are not the real-world majority. The quiet majority doesn't post.
⏸️ Emotional firewallThe angrier a post makes you instantly, the more that anger might be the point. Let the bullet fly a while.
🔍 Source hygieneAccounts that only post attacks, never sleep, or use stolen profile photos — those are the tells.
🚫 Starve the spreadAn angry reply is still engagement. Not feeding the algorithm is the strongest counterattack.
